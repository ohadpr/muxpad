Pinned cards: five ways to stop paying 34px for a label

Measured on the live Investing chat: the card is 78px tall — a 34px header and a 42px body. For a one-line card, 44% of it is chrome, and the header says two things you mostly already know.

Each option below is shown three ways, because they fail differently: one line (the complaint), three lines (does it still work?), and two stacked (does the stack stay readable?).

Inline — the name is a prefix, not a row

−34px on one-liners

Chevron, name, content and age share one line. When content wraps past one line it drops to its own row and the name stays up top as a label — so a long card degrades into roughly what exists today, and a short one costs a single row.

Cost: the name and the age eat horizontal room from the content, which is exactly the dimension a one-line market card is already fighting for on a phone.

Reveal — no chrome at rest

−34px always

The card is only its content, with a 3px accent rail down the left edge saying "this is pinned, not a message". Name and age fade in on hover. The rail turns amber when the writer has gone overdue, and a dot appears, so the one fact worth interrupting you for still reaches you without a row.

Cost: no hover on a phone — the name becomes long-press-only, and the stack of a multi-card chat turns into anonymous blocks. Hover over the cards to see it.

Notch — the label rides on the border

−27px

A fieldset legend. The name and age sit on the card's top border line, interrupting it, so they occupy a line that already existed. Both stay visible at all times, on every device, with no hover and no tap.

Cost: small type (10.5px), and the labels need a background matching whatever is behind the card — which breaks the moment the transcript scrolls underneath, since the card floats over the log.

Rail — turn the chrome sideways

−34px vertical

Spend the scarce dimension you have (width) instead of the one you don't (height). A 26px gutter holds the name vertically; the age tucks into the content's bottom-right. Scales perfectly — a card is as tall as its content and not one pixel more.

Cost: 26px of every line's width, forever, and rotated text is genuinely slower to read. On a 430px phone that is 6% of the pane gone to a label.

Monogram — the name shrinks to a chip

−34px always

A 20px square carrying the card's first letters, inline with the content. It is the identity, the collapse toggle and the staleness signal in one object: amber when overdue. Full name and age on tap or hover. Costs ~30px of width and zero height.

Cost: mk is not market — with two cards you learn the glyphs, with six you are decoding. And the age disappears from view entirely.

Welded — the card is the composer's top edge

−34px, and −8px of gap

Not a separate object at all. The card shares the composer's surface and border, sitting on its top edge like a toolbar — so it costs its content line and nothing else: no second border, no shadow, no gap between two floating slabs. The name is inline, dimmed, because it is a label on a strip you already know is pinned.

Cost: it stops reading as "a thing an agent wrote" and starts reading as muxpad's own chrome — and it only works for one or two cards; a stack of six welded to the composer is a wall.

If you want a recommendation

A (inline), with C's instinct as the fallback. It is the only one that removes the whole row while keeping the name and the staleness permanently visible, on every device, with no hover and no decoding — and it degrades honestly: the moment content needs two lines it becomes what you have today, which is the right shape for a card that big.

The real question is what the header is for. The name matters when a chat has several cards; the age only matters when it has gone wrong. So the strongest version is A with the age suppressed until it is overdue — then a healthy one-line card is one line with a name on it, and the moment a writer stops, the card grows its warning back.

D is the only one that is correct at every content height, and I would still not ship it: 26px of every phone line is a steep standing charge for a label you read twice a day. B and E both trade a fact you can see for a gesture you must perform, which is the wrong trade for a surface whose entire job is being glanceable.
